# Deep Dive into Text Representation: From TF-IDF to Word Embeddings

This document provides an in-depth exploration of foundational concepts in Natural Language Processing (NLP) that bridge the gap between raw text and machine learning models. We will cover **Term Frequency (TF)**, **Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)**, **TF-IDF**, their strengths and weaknesses, and then move on to **word embeddings** with a focus on **Word2Vec**. A hands‑on example with a custom dataset and a toy feature‑based vector space will illustrate the evolution from sparse, count‑based representations to dense, semantic vectors.

---

## 1. The Classic Trio: TF, IDF and TF‑IDF

When we feed text to an algorithm, the first step is to convert words or tokens into numbers. A simple yet powerful scheme is the **Bag‑of‑Words (BoW)** model, where each document is represented by the counts (or weighted counts) of its terms. TF‑IDF is one of the most popular weighting methods within BoW.

### 1.1 A Toy Corpus

Let’s create a small dataset with three columns: **Document ID**, **Text**, and a **Label** (for potential classification tasks; label is not used in TF‑IDF calculation but included as requested).

| DocID | Text                    | Label |
|-------|-------------------------|-------|
| D1    | student watch mobile   | -1    |
| D2    | student love mobile    |  1    |
| D3    | student watch laptop   | -1    |
| D4    | teacher watch mobile   |  1    |

We have 4 documents (`N = 4`). After standard pre‑processing (lowercasing, tokenisation) the vocabulary is:  
`student`, `watch`, `mobile`, `love`, `laptop`, `teacher` (6 unique terms).

---

## 2. Term Frequency (TF)

Term Frequency measures how often a term appears in a document. Because documents vary in length, we normalise by the total number of terms in that document.

**Equation (normalised TF):**

$$
\text{TF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{count of term } t \text{ in document } d}{\text{total number of terms in } d}
$$

### 2.2 Computing TF for the Full Table

First, the raw term counts per document:

| Term      | D1 | D2 | D3 | D4 |
|-----------|----|----|----|----|
| student   | 1  | 1  | 1  | 0  |
| watch     | 1  | 0  | 1  | 1  |
| mobile    | 1  | 1  | 0  | 1  |
| love      | 0  | 1  | 0  | 0  |
| laptop    | 0  | 0  | 1  | 0  |
| teacher   | 0  | 0  | 0  | 1  |
| **Total** | 3  | 3  | 3  | 3  |

Now, divide each entry by the document total (3 for all here) to get the TF matrix:

| Term      | D1        | D2        | D3        | D4        |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| student   | 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 0.00      |
| watch     | 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 0.00      | 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 1/3 ≈ 0.33|
| mobile    | 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 0.00      | 1/3 ≈ 0.33|
| love      | 0.00      | 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 0.00      | 0.00      |
| laptop    | 0.00      | 0.00      | 1/3 ≈ 0.33| 0.00      |
| teacher   | 0.00      | 0.00      | 0.00      | 1/3 ≈ 0.33|

## 3. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

TF alone gives equal weight to all terms. However, words like “the” or “is” appear in nearly every document and carry little discriminative power. IDF reduces the influence of such frequent terms and boosts rare, potentially more informative ones.

**Equation (smooth IDF is often used, but we use the basic form):**

$$
\text{IDF}(t) = \log\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right)
$$

where:
- $N$ = total number of documents (4)
- $\text{df}(t)$ = number of documents that contain term $t$

### 3.1 Compute Document Frequencies and IDF

| Term      | df(t) | IDF(t) = log(4 / df(t)) |
|-----------|-------|--------------------------|
| student   | 3     | log(1.333) ≈ 0.2877      |
| watch     | 3     | log(1.333) ≈ 0.2877      |
| mobile    | 3     | log(1.333) ≈ 0.2877      |
| love      | 1     | log(4)     ≈ 1.3863      |
| laptop    | 1     | log(4)     ≈ 1.3863      |
| teacher   | 1     | log(4)     ≈ 1.3863      |

All logarithms are natural logs (base \( e \)).

---

## 4. TF‑IDF: Putting It Together

The final TF‑IDF weight for a term in a document is simply the product of TF and IDF:

$$
\text{TF‑IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t)
$$

### 4.1 TF‑IDF Matrix for Our Corpus

Multiply each TF cell by the corresponding term’s IDF:

| Term      | D1                  | D2                  | D3                  | D4                  |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| student   | 0.33 × 0.2877 ≈ 0.0959 | 0.0959             | 0.0959             | 0.0                 |
| watch     | 0.33 × 0.2877 ≈ 0.0959 | 0.0                | 0.0959             | 0.0959             |
| mobile    | 0.0959              | 0.0959             | 0.0                | 0.0959             |
| love      | 0.0                 | 0.33 × 1.3863 ≈ 0.4621 | 0.0                | 0.0                 |
| laptop    | 0.0                 | 0.0                | 0.4621             | 0.0                 |
| teacher   | 0.0                 | 0.0                | 0.0                | 0.4621             |

Notice how rare words (`love`, `laptop`, `teacher`) receive much higher weights in the documents where they appear than the common word `student`, even though all raw counts were 1.

## 5. Advantages and Disadvantages

### 5.1 Term Frequency (TF) alone
- **Advantage:** Extremely simple; captures the prominence of a term inside a document.
- **Disadvantage:** All terms are treated equally; stop‑word‑like frequent words (e.g., “is”, “the”) dominate the representation and hide meaningful content.

### 5.2 Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) alone
- **Advantage:** Reflects how “specific” a word is across the collection; common words are naturally penalised.
- **Disadvantage:** Used by itself, IDF does not account for how many times a word appears in one document. A term that occurs once in a document is treated the same as one that occurs 50 times.

### 5.3 TF‑IDF (Combined)
**Advantages:**
- Balances local frequency (TF) with global rarity (IDF) – a robust heuristic for information retrieval and text classification.
- Computationally cheap, no training required.
- Interpretable: we can explain why a document is relevant to a query.

**Disadvantages:**
- Still a **sparse**, high‑dimensional representation (vocabulary size often in the millions).
- Ignores **word order** and context (bag‑of‑words assumption).
- No notion of **semantic similarity** – “mobile” and “phone” are completely unrelated vectors.
- Cannot handle out‑of‑vocabulary words without rebuilding the whole matrix.

These drawbacks motivate **word embeddings**, our next topic.

## 6. Word Embeddings: Dense Semantic Vectors

A **word embedding** is a learned, dense vector representation of a word, typically in a low‑dimensional continuous space (e.g., 100–300 dimensions). Unlike TF‑IDF vectors which are sparse and orthogonal, embeddings capture semantic and syntactic relationships: similar words are close together, and analogies like `king - man + woman ≈ queen` hold.

### 6.1 Why Use Word Embeddings?
1. **Dimensionality reduction** – from vocab‑size to a few hundred floats.
2. **Semantic similarity** – cosine distance between vectors reflects meaning.
3. **Generalisability** – embeddings learned from large corpora transfer well to other tasks (pre‑training).
4. **Capturing relationships** – vector arithmetic works: `Russia + river ≈ Volga`, or `Paris - France + Italy ≈ Rome`.
5. **Better input for neural networks** – dense vectors allow models to learn smoothly.

### 6.2 Word2Vec: Learning Embeddings from Context

*Word2Vec* (Mikolov et al., 2013) is a pioneering method that learns word vectors by predicting context. Two flavours exist:

#### Continuous Bag‑of‑Words (CBOW)
- **Goal:** Predict the target word from a window of surrounding context words.
- Example: given *“The __ barks at the stranger”*, predict *“dog”*.
- The model sums/averages the embeddings of context words and uses a softmax over the vocabulary.

#### Skip‑gram
- **Goal:** Predict surrounding context words given the target word.
- Example: from *“dog”* predict *“The”, “barks”, “at”, “the”, “stranger”*.
- Works better for small datasets and captures rare words well.

#### Training tricks
- **Negative sampling:** Instead of updating all output weights, only a few “negative” (random) words are updated, making training far more efficient.
- **Hierarchical softmax:** Uses a tree structure to speed up probability computation.

Word2Vec produces two sets of vectors (input and output); usually the input vectors are taken as final embeddings.

### 6.3 Example: Semantic vector space with toy features

To understand how vectors can encode meaning, let’s manually create a small feature‑based representation for five words. We define numeric columns for the features: **gender** (scale from -1 = masculine to +1 = feminine), **wealth**, **power**, **weight**, and **speak** (1 = can speak, 0 = cannot).

| Feature   | king | queen | man | woman | monkey |
|-----------|------|-------|-----|-------|--------|
| gender    | -0.9 |  0.9  | -1.0|  1.0  |  0.0   |
| wealth    |  1.0 |  0.9  |  0.2|  0.2  |  0.0   |
| power     |  1.0 |  0.8  |  0.4|  0.3  |  0.1   |
| weight    |  0.8 |  0.6  |  0.7|  0.5  |  0.3   |
| speak     |  1   |  1    |  1  |  1    |  0     |

Each column is the **word vector** for that term, e.g.:
- **king**   = [-0.9, 1.0, 1.0, 0.8, 1]
- **queen**  = [ 0.9, 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 1]
- **man**    = [-1.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.7, 1]
- **woman**  = [ 1.0, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1]
- **monkey** = [ 0.0, 0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 0]

#### 6.3.1 Semantic Similarity using Cosine Similarity

The cosine similarity between two vectors \( A \) and \( B \) is:

$$
\text{cosine}(A, B) = \frac{A \cdot B}{\|A\| \, \|B\|}
$$

We compute it for some interesting pairs.

**king vs queen:**
- dot = (-0.9×0.9) + (1.0×0.9) + (1.0×0.8) + (0.8×0.6) + (1×1) = -0.81 + 0.9 + 0.8 + 0.48 + 1 = 2.37
- ||king|| = sqrt(0.81 + 1 + 1 + 0.64 + 1) = sqrt(4.45) ≈ 2.109
- ||queen|| = sqrt(0.81 + 0.81 + 0.64 + 0.36 + 1) = sqrt(3.62) ≈ 1.903
- similarity = 2.37 / (2.109 × 1.903) ≈ 2.37 / 4.013 ≈ **0.590**

**king vs man:**
- dot = (-0.9×-1.0) + (1.0×0.2) + (1.0×0.4) + (0.8×0.7) + (1×1) = 0.9 + 0.2 + 0.4 + 0.56 + 1 = 3.06
- ||man|| = sqrt(1 + 0.04 + 0.16 + 0.49 + 1) = sqrt(2.69) ≈ 1.640
- similarity = 3.06 / (2.109 × 1.640) ≈ 3.06 / 3.459 ≈ **0.884**  
  (king and man are very similar – both masculine, but man is less wealthy/powerful)

**queen vs woman:**
- dot = (0.9×1.0) + (0.9×0.2) + (0.8×0.3) + (0.6×0.5) + (1×1) = 0.9 + 0.18 + 0.24 + 0.30 + 1 = 2.62
- ||woman|| = sqrt(1 + 0.04 + 0.09 + 0.25 + 1) = sqrt(2.38) ≈ 1.542
- similarity = 2.62 / (1.903 × 1.542) ≈ 2.62 / 2.934 ≈ **0.893**  
  (similarity between queen and woman is high)

**man vs monkey:**
- dot = (-1×0) + (0.2×0) + (0.4×0.1) + (0.7×0.3) + (1×0) = 0 + 0 + 0.04 + 0.21 + 0 = 0.25
- ||monkey|| = sqrt(0 + 0 + 0.01 + 0.09 + 0) = sqrt(0.10) ≈ 0.316
- similarity = 0.25 / (1.640 × 0.316) ≈ 0.25 / 0.518 ≈ **0.483**  
  (much lower; monkey lacks speech, gender, wealth, power)

**king vs monkey:**
- dot = (-0.9×0) + (1×0) + (1×0.1) + (0.8×0.3) + (1×0) = 0 + 0 + 0.1 + 0.24 + 0 = 0.34
- similarity = 0.34 / (2.109 × 0.316) ≈ 0.34 / 0.666 ≈ **0.510**

These numbers confirm what we expect: royalty pairs are close to their human counterparts, the monkey is far from everyone, and the famous *King – Man + Woman ≈ Queen* relationship is hinted at (king vector – man vector + woman vector = [-0.9-(-1)+1, 1-0.2+0.2, 1-0.4+0.3, 0.8-0.7+0.5, 1-1+1] = [1.1, 1.0, 0.9, 0.6, 1], which is very close to our queen vector [0.9, 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 1]).

Real‑world embeddings like those from Word2Vec are trained on billions of words and learn such feature dimensions automatically, without human design. The resulting vector space exhibits these linear regularities and semantic clusters, making them invaluable for modern deep learning in NLP.

---

## 7. Summary

- **TF** counts words locally; **IDF** down‑weighs globally common terms; together they give **TF‑IDF**, a strong sparse baseline.
- **TF‑IDF** is fast and interpretable but cannot capture meaning beyond term frequency.
- **Word embeddings** (like Word2Vec) project discrete words into a low‑dimensional, dense space where distance encodes semantic similarity.
- With embeddings, models can understand analogies, synonyms, and contextual relationships, forming the foundation for recurrent neural networks, transformers, and virtually all modern NLP architectures.
